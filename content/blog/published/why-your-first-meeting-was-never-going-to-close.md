If you've sold into Japan for more than a quarter, you know the pattern: a great first meeting, warm nods, someone says "very interesting," and then six weeks of silence. Founders and VPs of Sales who are new to the market usually read that silence as rejection, or as a signal the product didn't land. Most of the time it means the opposite — the meeting worked exactly as intended, and the actual decision process hasn't started yet.

## The meeting you had wasn't the meeting you thought it was

In most Western enterprise sales cycles, the first meeting is where interest gets tested and a next step gets negotiated. In Japan, the first meeting with an individual contributor or mid-level manager is frequently an information-gathering exercise on their end — not because they're not empowered, but because their job at that stage is to accurately represent your pitch to people who weren't in the room, not to react to it in real time.

That's why direct questions like "does this look interesting to you?" often get polite non-answers. The person across the table may genuinely be interested and still not be the one positioned to say so.

## Ringi and nemawashi aren't bureaucracy — they're risk management

Ringi-sho (the formal approval document) and nemawashi (the informal groundwork done before a decision is floated) get treated in a lot of Japan market-entry content as quaint bureaucratic friction to route around. That's a mistake. These processes exist to make sure that by the time a decision is formally proposed, everyone who could object already agrees — which means the visible "decision" is often the last step of a process that's been running invisibly for weeks.

If you're only tracking the visible steps — meetings, calls, a proposal — you're missing most of the actual sales cycle. The real work of the deal is happening in conversations you're not in.

## What this changes about how you sell

A few practical implications:

- Don't over-index on the emotional temperature of a single meeting. A flat-affect meeting can still be moving forward internally; an enthusiastic one can still die quietly if nemawashi never happens.
- Ask directly, but tactfully, who else needs to be comfortable with this before it moves — not "who's the decision maker," which often gets an evasive answer, but "who else on the team should we make sure has the context."
- Build in more calendar time than your home-market forecast model assumes. A deal that looks stalled at week six of silence may be exactly on schedule for a Japan enterprise cycle.

## The takeaway

Silence after a good first meeting in Japan is not, by itself, a signal to worry about. It's frequently a signal that the deal has moved into the part of the process you can't see — and the sales motion that works here is one built to support that invisible groundwork, not fight against it.
