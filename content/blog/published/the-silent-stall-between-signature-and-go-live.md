We've covered [the stall that happens before a pilot converts to a contract](/blog/the-pilot-that-went-nowhere), and [the one that happens at renewal after a quiet, under-supported onboarding](/blog/the-japan-renewal-cliff-nobody-forecasted). There's a third stall in between those two, and it's easy to miss because nobody's tracking it as a distinct stage: the gap between a signed contract and the customer actually going live.

## Why this gap runs longer in Japan than a global onboarding timeline assumes

Most SaaS onboarding thinking is built around time-to-value — get the customer to a meaningful first outcome as fast as possible, because the first weeks are where churn risk is highest. That logic is sound, and it assumes the customer can start onboarding close to immediately after signature.

In a Japanese enterprise account, signature often isn't the starting gun HQ thinks it is. The vendor contract itself frequently needs to clear internal procurement review, IT security assessment, and sometimes a separate internal approval process distinct from the one that got the deal signed in the first place — the commercial decision and the operational green light to actually start using the product are not always the same approval. A deal can be legally signed and commercially "won" while the customer's own internal process for actually turning the product on is still weeks away from clearing.

## Why this becomes a silent risk, not a visible one

A global onboarding motion built around a fast time-to-value clock starts counting from signature. If the customer's internal process hasn't cleared yet, early onboarding touchpoints — welcome emails, kickoff scheduling, usage-activation nudges — land against an account that isn't actually able to act on them yet. From the vendor side, this can look like early disengagement or a slow-starting customer. It's often neither; it's an account still waiting on its own internal clearance.

The risk is that a vendor reads this delay as a customer-side problem (poor internal champion, low priority) and either backs off or pushes harder in ways that don't address the actual blocker, when what's needed is simply an accurate read of where the account is in its own internal process — which is usually a security/procurement timeline, not a product-adoption one.

## What this looks like handled well

- **Ask about internal security and procurement review timelines as part of the sales process, before signature — not as a surprise afterward.** Knowing this upfront lets you set an accurate go-live expectation with your own internal stakeholders too, instead of reporting a "signed" deal as though onboarding starts the same week.
- **Don't run your standard time-to-value onboarding clock against an account that hasn't cleared its own internal gate yet.** Early automated nudges aimed at a champion who's still waiting on IT security sign-off read as tone-deaf, not helpful.
- **Use the pre-go-live gap productively rather than passively.** This is a good window for the champion to keep building the internal case with stakeholders who'll be affected once the product goes live — the same ongoing nemawashi that closed the deal doesn't stop just because the contract is signed.

## The takeaway

A signed Japan contract and a live Japan customer are not the same milestone, and the gap between them is often longer than a global onboarding motion assumes — for internal-process reasons that have nothing to do with product fit or champion enthusiasm. Track that gap as its own stage, with its own expectations, instead of letting a fast global time-to-value clock quietly misread a normal procurement delay as an early warning sign.
