We've written before about [why a foreign e-commerce pitch built around "help merchants go direct-to-consumer" tends to underperform in Japan](/blog/the-mall-effect-ecommerce-in-japan), because the marketplace is doing real trust-building work a standalone storefront hasn't earned. The same pattern shows up, almost unchanged, in Japanese travel and hospitality — with Rakuten Travel and Jalan playing the role Rakuten Ichiba plays in retail.

## The OTA layer isn't just distribution here

Rakuten Travel and Jalan together dominate Japan's domestic accommodation booking market, and both are deeply embedded in their parent companies' broader ecosystems — Rakuten's points and cross-service loyalty program on one side, Jalan's tie to Recruit's wider consumer platforms on the other. For a domestic traveler choosing between an unfamiliar ryokan and a well-reviewed one, booking through a platform they already trust does real work that a hotel's own site can't replicate on short notice: it pre-validates the property.

That's structurally the same dynamic as the Mall Effect in retail — the platform is often the trusted party, not the merchant, especially for a first-time guest with no prior relationship to the property.

## Why "book direct and cut out the OTA" lands differently here

A foreign hospitality-tech pitch built around "help properties recover margin by driving direct bookings" is a reasonable pitch in a lot of markets. In Japan, it risks asking a property to give up the part of the funnel that's actually earning it first-time guests. Direct-booking tooling isn't wrong — but leading with disintermediation, the way the pitch often runs in less OTA-dependent markets, undersells what the OTA relationship is actually doing for an unfamiliar property.

## What this means for the pitch

- **Lead with what the tooling does inside the OTA relationship, not instead of it** — rate parity management, review and reputation tooling that strengthens the property's standing on Rakuten Travel/Jalan itself, or guest-data capture that works alongside OTA bookings rather than replacing them.
- **Reserve the direct-booking pitch for properties that already have brand recognition or a strong repeat-guest base** — an established ryokan with loyal returning guests has less to gain from OTA-driven discovery than a first-time entrant does.
- **Treat NPS and review-score visibility on the major OTAs as a real lever, not a vanity metric.** Guest decision-making in Japan runs heavily through platform reputation, so tooling that improves a property's OTA-side standing is closer to the actual buying trigger than direct-booking conversion tooling is.

## The takeaway

Japanese travel distribution runs on the same trust logic as Japanese e-commerce: the platform, not the property, is often what a first-time guest is actually trusting. A pitch built around escaping that dependency is fighting the market's structure. One built around strengthening a property's position within it is working with the structure — and lands better with hospitality operators who aren't looking to replace their OTA relationships, just to get more out of them.
