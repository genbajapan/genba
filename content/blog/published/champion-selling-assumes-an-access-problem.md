Champion-selling content is everywhere in sales circles this year: find the internal advocate, arm them with proof points, help them build consensus. The underlying logic makes sense — the more stakeholders a deal touches, the more a deal depends on someone inside the account doing work you can't do yourself. For a foreign SaaS company selling into Japan, that logic is right. The default picture of what a champion actually *does* is where it needs translation — the same [nemawashi mechanics behind why a good first meeting goes quiet](/blog/why-your-first-meeting-was-never-going-to-close) are exactly what a champion is running on your behalf.

## What the standard playbook assumes a champion is for

Most champion-selling frameworks are built around an access problem: a large buying committee, a lot of stakeholders you haven't met, and a champion whose main value is opening doors — getting you in front of the economic buyer, the technical evaluator, procurement, legal. Equip the champion with the right proof and messaging, and they clear your path to the people who actually decide.

That model assumes the vendor's job is to eventually reach everyone who matters, with the champion as the connector.

## Why that model doesn't transfer cleanly to Japan

In a Japanese enterprise account, you often never meet most of the people your champion needs to bring along. That's not a failure of the relationship — it's the actual mechanism. Nemawashi is the champion building agreement across the organization on your behalf, in conversations you were never going to be invited into, using arguments shaped for internal audiences rather than the version you'd give them yourself.

The champion's job here isn't primarily "get the vendor access." It's "make the internal case land with people the vendor will likely never speak to directly." That's a fundamentally different skill and a fundamentally different kind of support to provide.

## What this changes about how you support a champion

- **Give them internally-usable material, not vendor-facing material.** A slide deck built to impress a technical evaluator in a live demo is a different artifact from a one-pager a champion can forward, unedited, to a department head who will never take your call.
- **Stop measuring champion strength by how many stakeholders they've introduced you to.** In many Japan deals, a strong champion introduces you to *fewer* people, not more, because they're absorbing the internal consensus-building themselves rather than routing it through you.
- **Ask what they need to make their internal case, not what they need from a product standpoint.** Those are often different questions. The second one gets you feature requests. The first one gets you the actual blocker.

## The takeaway

This year's champion-selling advice is directionally right — deals with a strong internal advocate close more often and faster, in Japan as much as anywhere. But the standard picture of what a champion does for you is built around an access problem. In Japan, translate it: your champion's real job is building consensus you'll never personally witness, and the support that helps them do that looks different from the support built for a market where you eventually meet the whole committee yourself.
